# Show the steps

An action-led explainer: the viewer sees the action that earns each instruction.
This is a recipe for choosing and arranging footage, not a fixed template.

## Purpose and driver

Choose one bounded task or subtask the supplied footage can actually explain.
Picture carries the procedure; relevant recorded speech can explain the reason.
Without speech, short observational labels can name visible stages. They are
graphics, not invented spoken captions. Let the subject determine length,
framing, type, colours, and whether any drawn theme helps.

## Footage and sequence

Log the real order, the hand/tool contact, and the result of each chosen action.
Prefer a coherent object and one readable action at a time. An establishing
view can locate the work before a detail proves it. Keep enough approach and
completion to understand the action; remove repetition that adds no information.
An omitted stage must not turn an overview into an apparently complete tutorial.

In a portrait video, choose crops on the work, hands and tool together. Where
they cannot share a crop, an intentionally composed wider view or inset is
better than hiding the contact point. Inspect the worst moments, not just a
good still. Place the instruction beside its action, with reading time and
clear space. Number only genuine steps whose order matters.

When a scope qualification changes the promise (for example, selected stages
rather than a complete lesson), give it phone-readable size and contrast too.
Do not hide that qualification in a faint footnote while the headline makes
the stronger claim.

## Words and sound

Use the person's verified names, measurements and cautions only. A visible
demonstration alone does not establish safe technique or a required measurement.
Use concise, legible wording on the corresponding action, not decorative cards
that replace the evidence. Captions transcribe actual speech only. Cleared work
sound, relevant speech, quiet music or silence are options; music must not hide
an audible cue. Never record a microphone or invent workshop ambience.

## Must not

Do not invent missing instructions, safety advice, results, speech or continuity.
Do not substitute a later object for the earlier one without disclosure. Do not
call a partial process a complete lesson, or wet clay a fired or usable product.

## Review and evidence

- [RES, context-limited] Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014), *How video production affects
  student engagement*, observational edX study: https://juhokim.com/files/LAS2014-Engagement.pdf.
  Focus and legibility are worth testing; this does not establish a Shorts
  duration, teaching effectiveness, retention or sales benefit.
- [ITE, practitioner] `skills/letitedit-modes/references/tutorial-explainer.md`
  and its `sources.md`: purpose-led coverage and comprehension time. Practitioner
  advice is not an independent effectiveness trial.
- [GEN] The bounded task, action/label alignment and crop choices above are craft
  proposals. Judge the whole export at 1 fps and in motion; check labels against
  the source, sound, ending, and the actual brief. Local checks are [OURS], not
  audience research. Pass the recipe and limitations to the separate reviewer.

## Source and delivery limits

Check qualifications at phone size. A partial or already edited demonstration is not a complete lesson or proof of raw-take selection. Keep object identity and process stages honest. Silence establishes no sound-design result. Retain cleared operation sound or disclose its absence, mute uncleared music, preserve required source and licence notices, and verify people and marketing clearances before public use.
